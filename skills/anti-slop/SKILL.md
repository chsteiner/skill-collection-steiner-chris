# Anti-Slop

Calibrated for the Claude Opus and Fable model families. In recent Opus output the old marker words and the em dashes have largely gone. What stays, and in part grows, is structure: formatting where prose belongs, bold-lead bullets, contrast figures, announced importance, slogan and summary endings, invented detail. Fable shares most of these. In the counted Fable texts dashes are rare and semicolons more frequent than in recent Opus text, and in reports after long work its prose can run dense, with labels it coined along the way. The rules describe mechanisms. Their examples show what a hit looks like and are not a list of banned words. Measurements and sources: `references/basis.md`.

The master heuristic: look for clusters of patterns, not single hits. One contrast figure is writing. Three of them plus a bold-lead scaffold plus a slogan ending is slop. Exceptions: rules 6 and 8, the German "tragen" case of rule 9 and the coined labels of rule 10 count on a single hit in text you produce, and in model-generated text the closing line (rules 3 and 4) is a finding on its own. For human text, see Guardrails.

## Modes

**Self-check**: silent, while generating prose deliverables over ~200 words (reports, articles, emails, blog posts, documentation prose). Apply the rules before output, and don't show the check. Conversational chat replies are not deliverables, so leave them out.

**Audit**: when the user says "check for slop" (diagnose only) or "deslop" (diagnose and rewrite). An explicit audit request always runs, even on text types the self-check skips (someone may well say "deslop this commit message", so do it). Reply in the user's language and quote passages verbatim in their original language.

Audit output format:

1. Findings as a numbered list: quoted passage (or a section reference for document-wide findings such as over-formatting or a buried lede), rule number, suggested fix, one line each. Group repeated hits of the same rule into one finding with a count and one or two examples.
2. For "deslop" only: the full rewritten text after the findings. Before you hand it over, reread the rewrite itself against rules 3 and 4: rewrites tend to keep a second contrast or to end on a new one.
3. If the text is clean, say so in one sentence. Don't manufacture findings.

In both modes, fenced or inline code and verbatim quotations are exempt from all rules. The self-check also stays off for code, commit messages, terminal output, structured data, and quick drafts. "Just a quick draft" from the user overrides it entirely.

## The rules

1. **Format only what has structure.** Use headers, lists, tables or bold when the user asks for them, or when the content has structure a reader navigates: steps in order, parallel options to compare, reference data, a document type that expects it (runbook, FAQ, reference docs). Emails, essays, blog posts, reports written to be read through, and explanatory documentation are prose. The typical misfires are numbered section headers in an essay ("## 1. Your audience stops being local"), a "Bottom Line" or "Fazit" heading on a blog post, bold-lead paragraphs (`**Use existing tools.** You don't need to build software...`), bullets that each open with a bold label, and `---` separators. When you dissolve a list, give each point its own sentence or paragraph rather than one packed block.

2. **Break the triads.** Threes are the default scaffold: three reasons, three lessons, "First... Second... Third...", "Erstens... Zweitens... Drittens...", and summary sentences of the form "sichtbarer, vernetzter und effizienter". One triad may stay. For the rest, change the presentation, not the content: merge two points, run them as flowing argument, vary the scaffold. Never add or drop a substantive point just to escape the triad.

3. **Ration the contrast figure.** "not X, but Y" / "X, not Y" / "It isn't X. It's Y." / "This doesn't mean X. It means Y." / "X is more than a Y, it's a Z" / "rather than" / "nicht X, sondern Y" / "kein X, sondern Y". The quieter form counts too: two parallel sentences that set X against Y ("Code demands precision about implementation. Documentation demands clarity about purpose."). At most one per text, and never as the closing line. Watch for the unprompted alternative as well: saying what the text is *not* about, what you won't do, or what stays unchanged, when nobody asked.

4. **End on content.** No closing slogan or aphorism ("Write for the people first, and the code will follow." / "Unser Kalender wird es uns danken."), no German "Wer X, der Y" wisdom sentence, no summary closer that repeats what the text has said ("In short," / "The bottom line" / "Kurz gesagt:" / "Unterm Strich"), no comment-bait question ("Share your experiences in the comments" / "Schreibt es gern in die Kommentare"), and no call to action where the genre doesn't call for one. A company post may point to open roles. A museum blog post doesn't need to ask for comments.

5. **Cut announcement frames and hollow emphasis.** A frame tells the reader that something is coming or that it matters, instead of saying it. This covers colon reveals that stage a point ("Die gute Nachricht: ..." / "Here's the thing:"), announced importance ("This matters because...", also as a heading), scene openers of the form "Wer X, kennt das Problem:", question-as-transition fragments ("The result? Chaos." / "Das Ergebnis? Weniger Bugs."), single-sentence paragraphs placed for drama, and hedge transitions ("It's worth noting", "Importantly", "That said" / "Erwähnenswert ist", "Dabei gilt"). A colon that opens the explanation or list its own clause has already set up ("Der Antrag braucht drei Unterlagen: ...") is plain punctuation, not a reveal. State the point itself, or the concrete fact that makes it matter. Hedge once, where the uncertainty is real, and not in every paragraph. Marker words: genuinely, quietly, deliberately in English, bewusst and spürbar in German. Flag marker words only at three or more occurrences, or two in one paragraph. The other patterns follow the cluster heuristic.

6. **No invented specificity.** Models fabricate precise details as authenticity props: visitor counts, minutes, town sizes, inventory numbers, years. In text you produce, never invent a number: if the fact isn't given, stay honestly general or mark the assumption. In audits and rewrites of someone else's text, keep their factual claims unchanged and flag suspicious precision as a finding instead of silently generalizing it. That includes exact figures in a "typical scene" (eight people, forty-five minutes) that a reader may take as data. If the scene is clearly marked as hypothetical, report it as an observation. In summaries, wording taken over from a source without quotation marks counts as well: quote it or put it in your own words.

7. **Start with the point, not the frame.** No thesis-statement openers that bury the hook in paragraph three, no reflex scene or analogy openers ("Imagine a restaurant..." / "Stellen Sie sich vor..." / "Stellen wir uns ... vor" / "Viele Entwicklerteams kennen dieses Muster:"). Put the most concrete, newest information first.

8. **Em dashes: zero in text you produce or rewrite.** No em dash and no spaced en dash as a thought-dash, in both languages: use a colon, comma, parentheses, or a new sentence. An unspaced en dash in a range ("1990–2000", "pp. 13–22") is not a thought-dash. In audits, list em-dash use as a finding (noting when it reads as deliberate authorial voice). A dash no longer tells you which model wrote a text: recent Opus versions have practically stopped using them in prose.

9. **Say it literally.** Mannered prose swaps a direct statement for a stock metaphor or flourish that displays the writer: "a dial worth turning" for "a parameter worth varying", "earns its keep" for "still matters", "load-bearing" for "essential", "ist Gold wert", "öffnet die Tür zu". When a literal phrase is available, use it. A metaphor that is the claim itself, chosen for this text, is not mannered prose.
   **German "tragen" as a metaphor of support: zero in German prose you produce or rewrite.** Opus uses "tragen" for anything that holds, justifies, bears weight, or survives: "trägt die Software jede weitere Prüfrunde", "die Begründung trägt nicht", "eine tragende Zahl", "was etwas getragen hat", "das trägt das Argument". "tragfähig" in the same sense counts too ("eine tragfähige Begründung"). "Tragweite" and "zum Tragen kommen" stay. It reads as translated "load-bearing" and marks a text as Opus-written on sight, especially in proposals (Anträge), reports, and emails. Name the actual relation instead: "hält stand", "übersteht", "reicht", "begründet", "stützt", "trifft zu", or rebuild the sentence around the real verb. Literal and fixed uses stay ("eine Tasche tragen", "Kosten tragen", "trägt den Titel", "Rechnung tragen"). In audits, list each metaphorical use as a finding. In rewrites, replace it.
   The neighbouring German counterparts of English Opus tics (settles, decisive, verdict, names, turns on) are marker words, not zero-tolerance: "benennt", "hängt an", "entscheidet" / "ist entscheidend", "das Urteil" in their abstract sense. Flag them like the marker words of rule 5.

10. **Write for a reader who wasn't there.** In reports, summaries and handoffs after a stretch of work: every term or abbreviation you made up during the work gets introduced or spelled out. No arrow chains ("A → B → fails"), no hyphen-stacked compounds, no references to reasoning the reader never saw. Each coined label left unexplained is a finding. Density follows the cluster heuristic: a paragraph that packs several claims into long sentences joined by semicolons is a hit, one long sentence is not.

## Guardrails: don't over-correct

- The cluster heuristic governs rules 1, 2, 5, 7, 9 (apart from "tragen"), the density part of rule 10, and the contrast count in rule 3: one hit is not a finding. Rules 6 and 8, the German "tragen" case of rule 9, the coined labels of rule 10 and the closing line are the stated exceptions. The German marker words at the end of rule 9 follow the rule 5 threshold.
- A single-hit rule asks for a rebuilt sentence, not a look-alike. Swapping "trägt" for "schultert" in a sentence that still stages the same load metaphor fixes nothing.
- Neither staccato nor wall. Three-word fragment chains are the same disease in reverse, and so is a paragraph that packs several claims into long sentences. Split a sentence that makes more than one claim, break a paragraph where the topic turns, and keep the connectors that do the arguing ("but", "because", "sondern" inside a real argument). Don't impose hard limits on sentence length: they make prose choppy.
- Context decides. A postmortem may be structured, a README may use bullets, a real ranking may be a list. Stripping structure the content needs is a failure too.
- Author voice protects what is not on the rules list: slang, register mixing, long sentences, unusual metaphors, deliberate repetition. Human writers use contrast figures, dashes, and punchlines as well, and a satirist's aphorism is not slop. Clustered rule patterns are still worth reporting in human-written text, but report them as observations and leave the call to the author. When unsure, keep it: a wrong cut damages the text, a missed one is just a missed fix.
- Translations and close paraphrases preserve the source's rhetorical patterns, including its triads and contrasts. Deslop the rendering only if the user asks for that.
- Don't fake humanity. No manufactured casualness, typos, or slang to "sound human".

## Out of scope

GPT-era word lists (delve, tapestry, testament, landscape, journey...) are not part of this skill: those words are rare in the counted Opus and Fable texts (`references/basis.md`). Text from other model families needs its own vocabulary check.
