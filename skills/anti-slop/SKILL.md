# Anti-Slop

Calibrated for Claude Opus 5.5, Claude Fable 5.1 and GPT-6 Astra (September 2026). In Claude's own output the old marker words and the em dashes have largely gone; formatting where prose belongs, contrast figures, slogan endings, announcement frames and invented detail have stayed. Anthropic reports that Fable 5.1 can write denser prose and recommends steering it away from mannered prose. OpenAI reports that GPT-6 Astra tends toward heavily formatted answers and recurring phrases; inside Codex, whose base instructions already steer against that, it formats little and rarely uses the classic contrast figure, and what remains are dashes, colon reveals, and quieter antitheses. Sources are in Basis.

The master heuristic: look for clusters of patterns, not single hits. One contrast figure is writing; three of them plus a bold-lead scaffold plus a slogan ending is slop. Exceptions: rules 6 and 8 and the German "tragen" case of rule 9 are zero-tolerance for text you produce, and in model-generated text the closing line (rules 3 and 4) is a finding on its own; for human text, see Guardrails.

## Modes

**Self-check**: silent, while generating prose deliverables over ~200 words (reports, articles, emails, blog posts, documentation prose). Apply the rules before output; don't show the check. Conversational chat replies are not deliverables; leave them out.

**Audit**: when the user says "check for slop" (diagnose only) or "deslop" (diagnose and rewrite). An explicit audit request always runs, even on text types the self-check skips (someone may well say "deslop this commit message"; do it). Reply in the user's language; quote passages verbatim in their original language.

Audit output format:

1. Findings as a numbered list: quoted passage (or a section reference for document-wide findings such as over-formatting or a buried lede), rule number, suggested fix, one line each. Group repeated hits of the same rule into one finding with a count and one or two examples.
2. For "deslop" only: the full rewritten text after the findings. Before you hand it over, reread the rewrite itself against rules 3 and 4: rewrites tend to keep a second contrast or to end on a new one.
3. If the text is clean, say so in one sentence. Don't manufacture findings.

In both modes, fenced or inline code and verbatim quotations are exempt from all rules. The self-check also stays off for code, commit messages, terminal output, structured data, and quick drafts; "just a quick draft" from the user overrides it entirely.

## The rules

1. **Format only what has structure.** Use headers, lists, tables or bold when the user asks for them, or when the content has structure a reader navigates: steps in order, parallel options to compare, reference data, a document type that expects it (runbook, FAQ, reference docs). Emails, essays, blog posts, reports written to be read through, and explanatory documentation are prose. The typical misfires are numbered section headers in an essay ("## 1. Your audience stops being local"), a "Bottom Line" or "Fazit" heading on a blog post, bold-lead paragraphs (`**Use existing tools.** You don't need to build software...`), bold labels on every list item, and `---` separators. When you dissolve a list, give each point its own sentence or paragraph rather than one packed block. A consistent Opus tell: outside LinkedIn posts, every measured blog post had section headers, in Opus 4.8, 5 and 5.5 alike.

2. **Break the triads.** Threes are the default scaffold: three reasons, three lessons, "First... Second... Third...", "Erstens... Zweitens... Drittens...", and summary sentences of the form "sichtbarer, vernetzter und effizienter". One triad may stay. For the rest, change the presentation, not the content: merge two points, run them as flowing argument, vary the scaffold. Never add or drop a substantive point just to escape the triad.

3. **Ration the contrast figure.** "not X, but Y" / "X, not Y" / "It isn't X. It's Y." / "This isn't about X. It's about Y." / "This doesn't mean X. It means Y." / "rather than" / "nicht X, sondern Y" / "kein X, sondern Y" / "nicht nur X, sondern auch Y". The quieter form counts too: two parallel sentences that set X against Y ("Code demands precision about implementation. Documentation demands clarity about purpose."). At most one per text, and never as the closing line. Watch for the unprompted alternative as well: saying what the text is *not* about, what you won't do, or what stays unchanged, when nobody asked. (Opus 5.5: "nicht/kein ... sondern" in 16 of 60 German texts. OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra guide names "X, not Y" and "This isn't about X. It's about Y.")

4. **End on content.** No closing slogan or aphorism ("Write for the people first, and the code will follow." / "Unser Kalender wird es uns danken."), no German "Wer X, der Y" wisdom sentence, no summary closer ("In short," / "The bottom line" / "Kurz gesagt:" / "Unterm Strich"), no comment-bait question ("Share your experiences in the comments" / "Schreibt es gern in die Kommentare"), and no call to action where the genre doesn't call for one. A company post may point to open roles; a museum blog post doesn't need to ask for comments. (Opus 5.5: every one of 9 English and 9 German blog posts outside company and LinkedIn posts ended this way.)

5. **Cut announcement frames and hollow emphasis.** Colon reveals that stage a point instead of making it ("Die gute Nachricht: ..." / "Was wir am meisten unterschätzt haben: ..." / "Here's the thing:"), scene openers of the form "Wer X, kennt das Problem:", question-as-transition fragments ("The result? Chaos." / "Das Ergebnis? Weniger Bugs."), single-sentence paragraphs placed for drama, and hedge transitions ("It's worth noting", "Importantly", "That said" / "Erwähnenswert ist", "Dabei gilt"). Marker words: genuinely, quietly, deliberately (Claude, fading in Opus 5.5); bewusst, spürbar (German Claude text); delve, foster, leverage (suppressed in OpenAI's own GPT-6 Astra prompt, so check them in OpenAI text). Flag marker words only at three or more occurrences, or two in one paragraph; the other patterns follow the cluster heuristic.

6. **No invented specificity.** Models fabricate precise details as authenticity props: visitor counts, minutes, town sizes, inventory numbers, years. In text you produce, never invent a number: if the fact isn't given, stay honestly general or mark the assumption. In audits and rewrites of someone else's text, keep their factual claims unchanged and flag suspicious precision as a finding instead of silently generalizing it. That includes exact figures in a "typical scene" (eight people, forty-five minutes) that a reader may take as data; if the scene is clearly marked as hypothetical, report it as an observation.

7. **Start with the point, not the frame.** No thesis-statement openers that bury the hook in paragraph three, no reflex scene or analogy openers ("Imagine a restaurant..." / "Stellen Sie sich vor..." / "Stellen wir uns ... vor" / "Viele Entwicklerteams kennen dieses Muster:"). Put the most concrete, newest information first.

8. **Em dashes: zero in text you produce or rewrite.** No em dash and no spaced en dash as a thought-dash, in both languages: use a colon, comma, parentheses, or a new sentence. In audits, list em-dash use as a finding (noting when it reads as deliberate authorial voice). Dashes no longer tell you which model wrote a text: Opus 5.5 has practically stopped using them in prose, while GPT-6 Astra in Codex still does (em dash in 15 of 40 English texts, spaced en dash in 18 of 40 German ones).

9. **Say it literally.** Mannered prose swaps a direct statement for a metaphor or flourish: "a dial worth turning" for "a parameter worth varying", "earns its keep" for "still matters", "load-bearing" for "essential", "ist Gold wert", "öffnet die Tür zu". The same goes for coined compound labels presented as established terms ("exact-head checks", "editorial-row layouts"). When a literal phrase is available, use it.
   **German "tragen" as a metaphor of support: zero in German prose you produce or rewrite.** Opus uses "tragen" for anything that holds, justifies, bears weight, or survives: "trägt die Software jede weitere Prüfrunde", "die Begründung trägt nicht", "eine tragende Zahl", "was etwas getragen hat", "das trägt das Argument". "tragfähig" in the same sense counts too ("eine tragfähige Begründung"); "Tragweite" and "zum Tragen kommen" stay. It reads as translated "load-bearing" and marks a text as Opus-written on sight, especially in proposals (Anträge), reports, and emails. Name the actual relation instead: "hält stand", "übersteht", "reicht", "begründet", "stützt", "trifft zu", or rebuild the sentence around the real verb. Literal and fixed uses stay ("eine Tasche tragen", "Kosten tragen", "trägt den Titel", "Rechnung tragen"). In audits, list each metaphorical use as a finding; in rewrites, replace it.
   The neighbouring German counterparts of the English Opus tics (settles, decisive, verdict, names, turns on, doing the work) are marker words, not zero-tolerance: "benennt", "hängt an", "entscheidet" / "ist entscheidend", "das Urteil", "leistet die Arbeit" in their abstract sense. Flag them like the marker words of rule 5, at three or more occurrences or two in one paragraph.

## Guardrails: don't over-correct

- The cluster heuristic governs rules 1, 2, 5, 7 and 9, and the contrast count in rule 3: one hit is not a finding. Rules 6 and 8, the German "tragen" case of rule 9 and the closing line are the stated exceptions; the German marker words at the end of rule 9 follow the rule 5 threshold.
- Neither staccato nor wall. Three-word fragment chains are the same disease in reverse; so is a paragraph that packs several claims into long sentences. Split a sentence that carries more than one claim, break a paragraph where the topic turns, and keep the connectors that carry the argument ("but", "because", "sondern" inside a real argument).
- Context decides. A postmortem may be structured, a README may use bullets, a real ranking may be a list. Stripping structure the content needs is a failure too.
- Author voice protects what is not on the rules list: slang, register mixing, long sentences, unusual metaphors, deliberate repetition. Human writers use contrast figures, dashes, and punchlines as well; a satirist's aphorism is not slop. Clustered rule patterns are still worth reporting in human-written text, but report them as observations and leave the call to the author. When unsure, keep it: a wrong cut damages the text, a missed one is just a missed fix.
- Translations and close paraphrases preserve the source's rhetorical patterns, including its triads and contrasts; deslop the rendering only if the user asks for that.
- Don't fake humanity. No manufactured casualness, typos, or slang to "sound human".

## Basis

Own cold-prompt corpus, September 2026: 280 texts from the same 40 English and German prompts (email, report, blog, documentation, misc), no style instructions; Opus 5.5 (120), Opus 5 (80), Opus 4.8 (80). Measured there: the formatting, contrast and marker-word rates above (endings hand-classified); em dashes in 1 of 60 English Opus 5.5 texts (an empty table cell) against 39 of 40 for Opus 5; GPT-era vocabulary (delve, tapestry, testament, journey) absent from all 140 English texts. GPT-6 Astra: the same 40 prompts twice each (80 texts) through Codex in its standard setup, anti-slop disabled; headers in 5 of 40 English texts, "X, not Y" in 2 of 40. Not measured: Fable 5.1 (usage limit at the time); its rules rest on Anthropic's "Prompting Claude Fable 5.1" guide (writing density, mannered prose, formatting in chat). OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra prompting guide supplies the slop-word and contrastive-framing list. "load-bearing" comes from developer logs on Opus 5 (Matsuoka, August 2026). Earlier basis, kept where it still holds: July 2026 corpus of Opus 4.x and Fable 5 (80 texts). Known side effect, measured on Opus 5.5 with this skill loaded: prose paragraphs get longer (English, 39 words without the skill, 58 with it), so the split instruction in the guardrails matters.

The German "tragen" case of rule 9: user observation on Opus-drafted German prose, November 2026, plus a count in a private German rules-and-docs repository drafted with Opus 5 and 5.5 (per commit co-author lines): 92 occurrences of "tragen" forms in 35,515 words (2.6 per 1,000) against a wordfreq baseline of 0.28 per 1,000 for the same forms, about 9x. Not a cold-prompt measurement, and the count does not separate literal from metaphorical uses. The marker words of rule 9 are German counterparts of entries in English Claude tic lists found in November 2026 (archiewood/claudeisms, claudisms.ai, smixs/awesome-claude-output-styles); their methods were not checked here. Four of the German forms were counted in the same repository against wordfreq with a word-token count: benennen about 15x, hängen about 9x (all forms, not only "hängt an"), entscheiden about 8x, Urteil about 4x. "leisten" in any form is not over-represented there (about 0.6x), the phrase "leistet die Arbeit" does not occur, and it stays on the list only as the counterpart of "doing the work". The repository is about measurement ("gemessen" itself about 150x), so part of these factors is topic, which is why they are markers and not zero-tolerance.
